# Understanding the Data Science Lifecycle: Question → Data → Insight

## 1. Explaining the Lifecycle

In data science, real work always begins with a clear **Question**, not with code or models. Starting with a well-defined question is critical because it keeps the entire project focused on something useful — otherwise we waste time exploring random things that don't matter to anyone.

**Data** acts as evidence. Before doing any analysis, we have to understand the data deeply: where it comes from, what each column really means, its quality, missing values, and any biases. Good understanding of data ensures our findings are trustworthy and actually answer the original question.

**Insights** emerge only after careful exploration. They are not just numbers or beautiful charts — they are meaningful patterns explained in plain language that help people make real decisions. The steps connect like this: the Question guides which Data we collect and study, proper Data understanding leads to smart exploration, and only then do actionable Insights appear.

## 2. Applying the Lifecycle to a Project Context

**Project Context**: Predicting factors affecting crop yield in Tamil Nadu (especially paddy/rice) to help local farmers reduce losses.

- **The Question**: How do rainfall, temperature, soil conditions, and farming practices influence paddy yield across districts in Tamil Nadu, and how can this information guide better crop planning?

- **Type of data needed**: District-level data including crop area, production, yield rate, rainfall, temperature, soil type, and fertilizer usage. This data can come from public sources like Kaggle datasets ("Comprehensive Tamil Nadu Agriculture Dataset"), Indian government agriculture portals, Tamil Nadu Season & Crop Reports, and weather records from the India Meteorological Department.

- **Useful Insight for decision-making**: For example, the insight might show that in low-rainfall seasons in southern districts, paddy yield drops significantly unless farmers use specific short-duration varieties or adjust fertilizer timing. This would help individual farmers decide what to plant and when, and help government officers plan support programs, irrigation advice, or seed distribution to minimize losses.
